Vaccination records in the patient's wallet

A vaccination record can be held by the patient rather than by a central database. This project implements that on swiyu, Switzerland's e-ID trust infrastructure. A practice writes an administered dose into an app on the patient's phone as a signed digital document. A travel clinic later asks whether the patient is protected, and receives four of the eighteen data items that document holds; the other fourteen are not transmitted. The code runs against the swiyu Sandbox and Swiss Profiles 1.0.

Terms

Terms used on this page

The words below have a narrow technical meaning in this field, and are used in that meaning throughout this page.

TermMeaning here
CredentialA digital document signed by the organisation that issued it, held on the patient's phone. One administered dose is one credential.
ClaimA single data item inside a credential, such as the date of a dose or the disease it targets. The immunization credential has eighteen.
WalletThe app that stores credentials and releases claims from them. The patient operates it.
IssuerThe organisation that signs and hands over a credential — here a practice, a pharmacy, an insurer or the Confederation.
VerifierThe organisation that asks for claims — here a travel clinic, a practice reception, a pharmacy or a survey.
Selective disclosureThe property that a verifier receives only the claims it asked for and is entitled to. The rest stay on the phone.
Status listA published list of bits, one pair per credential, saying whether it is still valid. It contains no patient data.

Background

What we learned from the past

meineimpfungen.ch held the national electronic vaccination record until 2021, when it was shut down after serious security failures. Several hundred thousand people lost access to their own vaccination history at once.

The platform was badly built, and that explanation is accurate as far as it goes. There is a second, structural point. A design in which one database holds everyone's record carries a failure mode that careful engineering does not remove: the database can be breached, defunded, or switched off, and each of those removes access for every person at the same moment.

The paper vaccination booklet has one property the platform never had: it survives the organisation that issued it.

The design constraint this project starts from

So the record here is a set of credentials in the patient's wallet. Each administered dose is its own credential, signed by whoever administered it. A practice that closes cannot take the record with it; neither can a platform that is wound up.

The mechanism

How credentials reach the wallet, and how claims leave it

Four organisations issue credentials into the wallet: the Confederation, a health insurer, a practice and a pharmacy. Each issues only what it is entitled to assert. Claims leave the wallet in answer to a specific question from a verifier, with the patient's consent, one question at a time. The only infrastructure the two sides share is the status list published by the Confederation's Base Registry, which records whether a credential is still valid and nothing else about it.

Selective disclosure

What each verifier receives

The immunization credential carries eighteen claims. What a verifier receives is decided by an entitlement attached to its registered role, and a query for claims outside that entitlement is refused at the point where the query is built — before it is sent to the wallet. The timing matters: once the wallet has answered, the data has left the phone, and any check applied after that point is a promise about how the recipient will behave.

4 / 18 claims released

For comparison: a paper vaccination booklet handed across a counter discloses everything on the page. A PDF exported from a portal discloses everything in the file. A registry lookup discloses everything the registry holds about the patient, and additionally records that the lookup happened.

Governance

Three checks the code runs before health data moves

The technical profile answers whether a message is well formed and correctly signed. It does not answer who is allowed to assert a clinical fact, who is allowed to ask for one, and whether a particular presentation should be accepted. Those three questions are implemented as functions, run in that order, and each records its reason whether it permits or refuses.

reviewIssuance()

Who may assert this?

A practice may issue an immunization because it holds the vaccinator role. An insurer may not. Refused before the request reaches the issuer.

reviewRequest()

Who may ask, and for what?

Claims outside the role's entitlement are refused at query construction. Protected fields — the AHV number — need their own grant, whatever credential carries them.

reviewPresentation()

Should this be accepted?

Status list first, then Trust Protocol 2.0 markers. A governed use case without authorisation is refused under every policy, because that rule is a MUST.

Some of the Swiss Profile's trust rules are stated at SHOULD level and cannot be satisfied in the Sandbox, because actors there have no identity onboarding. The Sandbox policy records each such rule as waived, and the journal shows which rule was relaxed and on what grounds. A demonstrator that deletes the rule instead reports the same success and leaves no trace that anything was skipped.

The journal holds claim names and no claim values. It therefore shows that an interaction stayed within the rules without becoming a second copy of the patient's data. A test asserts that no AHV number and no vaccine lot number reaches it.

Standards

Using the openEHR and HL7 FHIR models without a shared repository

openEHR and HL7 FHIR are normally adopted as a package: a set of shared information models that define what a vaccination record contains, together with a shared repository that some organisation operates and governs. This project adopts the information models and does not adopt the repository.

Every claim carries the FHIR element path it corresponds to and, where one exists, its openEHR archetype path. When a presentation arrives, the receiving system rebuilds the representation it already understands from the claims the holder released. That happens locally, with no clinical data repository and no FHIR server on either side of the exchange.

Two properties hold for every projection, and the code states them at the point where it performs one. First, a projection is derived and not authoritative: the signed credential remains the evidence. Second, a projection is legitimately partial: after selective disclosure a FHIR resource may carry findings and no patient name, so receiving systems have to treat a missing element as a valid outcome rather than an error. That second property is the main integration cost of this approach.

The blueprint

Eleven documented flows

A flow is a description of one complete exchange: who does what, in what order, under which protocol. Each of the eleven is a self-contained file with machine-readable front matter, a sequence diagram, the governance constraints that apply, the standardisation constraints that apply, and the questions it leaves open. They are written so that they can be moved into a trust flows repository and sit beside flows from unrelated sectors.

Seven of them are also modelled in LikeC4, in the same form as the DIDAS reference flow, so that they can move without being redrawn. Every step in the model carries the protocol, the data format and the eHealth Suisse rule behind it in its notes. The other 2026 showcase

openEHR Switzerland brought a second vaccination showcase to the same hackathon: FHIR intake in CH VACD format, persisted as an openEHR composition in a clinical data repository. openEHR Switzerland and HL7 Switzerland have since formed a joint working group to turn it into a reusable implementation blueprint. They raise the substantive objection to the design on this page: a vaccination record has to stay clinically usable for a lifetime, and a document exchanged at one moment is a different object from a record maintained over decades.

The 2024 project's own staging is where the two approaches meet. It described three stages: document-oriented "EPD 1.0", structured and server-based "EPD 2.0", structured and wallet-based "EPD 3.0". The openEHR showcase is a working stage 2 and this project is a working stage 3. Composition in both directions is already specified — a repository issuing credentials from the records it holds, and a presented credential projecting into the ingestion path that blueprint defines. Building that composition is the main item on the 2027 list.

Public health

Vaccination coverage monitoring in Switzerland

Switzerland measures vaccination coverage through the Swiss National Vaccination Coverage Survey, run since 1999 by the Epidemiology, Biostatistics and Prevention Institute (EBPI) at the University of Zurich with the Federal Office of Public Health and all 26 cantons. It samples randomly selected households of 2-, 8- and 16-year-olds on a three-year rolling cycle, and asks the family to post a copy of the child's vaccination record.

The data source is already the record the family holds. Coverage monitoring never depended on a central register — when meineimpfungen.ch closed in 2021, the survey carried on, because it was never reading from it.

What the photocopy discloses

Every dose, every date, the vaccinating physician, and usually the child's name. Then manual transcription, manual coding, and up to three contact attempts by post and telephone.

What a presentation discloses

Five claims — target disease, date, dose number, doses in series, vaccine code. No name, no birth date, no performer, no lot number. Structured, signed, already coded to SNOMED.

Of the eleven flows, this is the one where a credential presentation discloses less than the procedure it would replace. The survey needs no identifying claim, because it drew the household from the population register and therefore already holds the age band and the canton. Consent adds no new step either: households are invited by letter today and may decline today.

One methodological constraint governs the whole design. The wallet is a response channel and not a sampling frame. A survey that allowed holders to volunteer their credentials would measure the people who choose to volunteer, and that selection bias cannot be corrected from inside the sample. EBPI draws the sample from the register; the wallet only answers.

Unlinkability

Unlinkability means that the party receiving data cannot connect it to the person it came from, and cannot connect two separate submissions to each other. For a coverage survey it is the property that matters most: a response that can be traced back to a household turns the survey into a register of who replied and what they replied, which is exactly what this architecture exists to avoid.

The design addresses the first half. The invitation posted to the household carries a single-use credential holding the stratum — age band, canton, survey cycle — and no household identifier. It is presented together with the dose claims in one combined proof and is revoked once the response is accepted, using the same two bits on a public status list that make a prescription single-use in F-05. The survey therefore learns that a household in a given canton with an 8-year-old reported a given set of doses, and holds no identifier it could join that record to.

Record nothing that could be recorded

A verifier receives a signed presentation and is technically able to keep all of it. The rule here is that the analysis variables are extracted and everything else is discarded: the transcript, the credentials, the signatures, and the request metadata — timing, IP address, user agent, response ordering. In a sample this small, each of those is identifying.

Where unlinkability is not yet structural

The survey both issues the invitation and revokes it, so a retained mapping from invitation to posted address would re-link a response by timing. The same dose credentials presented in two cycles are also linkable to each other. Closing both requires either batch issuance or a zero-knowledge presentation, and neither is in place.

Until one of those two mechanisms is implemented, this flow is unlinkable by governance rule rather than by construction, which is the weaker guarantee. A zero-knowledge presentation would let the wallet prove that a valid, unused invitation exists without revealing which one, and would remove the timing correlation. The candidate scheme and its fit to this profile are tracked in issue 10, Longfellow ZK.

Open questions

What is not solved

No one can grant the roles yet

Nothing here deploys until some body can issue the trust statement that says "this DID is a practice authorised to vaccinate". The Confederation's onboarding verifies who an organisation is; it does not state what that organisation may do in healthcare, and no health-domain governance body exists to state it. The technology is therefore ready some distance ahead of the institutional arrangements. That is the principal finding of step 1, and no amount of code changes it.

Series across issuers

Dose 2 is given by a pharmacy that cannot see dose 1. Today the administering party asserts the dose number, so a wallet holding two "dose 1 of 3" credentials is possible. Resolving it needs a presentation at administration time or a shared series identifier.

The double-dispensing window

Between a prescription being presented and being revoked there is a window in which a second pharmacy could accept it. Paper has the same window and closes it physically.

"Protected against X" is an inference

The credential says which diseases a dose targets and when. Whether that amounts to protection depends on the schedule and elapsed time. Who is accountable for that inference is a clinical-safety question for the sector to settle.

Presentations are not unlinkable

A credential presented twice is the same credential, so two verifiers comparing notes can tell it is the same person. Batch issuance reduces this and is not used here, because with a dose series the claim values are themselves close to identifying. A zero-knowledge presentation would address it directly; the candidate is tracked in issue 10.

The susceptible cannot be enumerated

Coverage measurement survives, because Switzerland has never measured it from a register, as the section above sets out. What a decentralised record removes is the ability to act on named individuals: outbreak response, recall, and catch-up campaigns all require knowing who is behind on a series. A sample yields a rate, a register would yield a list, and a wallet yields neither. This follows from the design and is a policy question rather than an engineering one.
