A vaccination record that no one has to operate

Switzerland's e-ID trust infrastructure, swiyu, is built for credentials the holder keeps. This is what happens when you point it at health data: a practice records an administered dose into the patient's wallet, and a travel clinic later confirms protection — receiving four of the eighteen claims the credential holds, because the other fourteen never leave the phone. The problem this answers

Switzerland already ran the centralised version

meineimpfungen.ch held the national electronic vaccination record until 2021, when it was shut down after serious security failures. Several hundred thousand people lost access to their own vaccination history at once.

The conclusion usually drawn is that the platform was badly built. The more useful one is structural: a design in which one database holds everyone's record has a failure mode that careful engineering does not remove. It can be breached, defunded, or simply switched off — and when it is, everyone loses simultaneously.

The paper vaccination booklet has one property the platform never had: it survives the organisation that issued it.

The design constraint this project starts from

So the record here is a set of credentials in the patient's wallet. Each administered dose is its own credential, signed by whoever administered it. A practice that closes cannot take the record with it; neither can a platform that is wound up.

The mechanism

Four organisations, one wallet, no shared database

Credentials flow into the wallet from whoever is entitled to assert them, and out of it only in answer to a specific question. The one piece of shared infrastructure is the status list — a bit array published by the Confederation's Base Registry that says whether a credential is still valid, and nothing else about it.

The thesis, made operable

Pick who is asking. Watch what they get.

The immunization credential carries eighteen claims. What a verifier receives is not decided by their good behaviour, and not filtered after the fact — it is decided by an entitlement attached to their registered role, and the request is refused when the query is built. Once the wallet has answered, the data is out, and a check any later is only a promise.

4 / 18 claims released

A paper vaccination booklet handed across a counter discloses everything on the page. A PDF from a portal discloses everything in the file. A registry lookup discloses everything the registry holds, plus the fact that the lookup happened.

Governance

Three gates, enforced in the flow

The technical profile answers "can this message be validated". It does not answer the questions that actually decide whether health data should change hands. Those live in code, run in order, and record their reasons either way.

reviewIssuance()

Who may assert this?

A practice may issue an immunization because it holds the vaccinator role. An insurer may not. Refused before the request reaches the issuer.

reviewRequest()

Who may ask, and for what?

Claims outside the role's entitlement are refused at query construction. Protected fields — the AHV number — need their own grant, whatever credential carries them.

reviewPresentation()

Should this be accepted?

Status list first, then Trust Protocol 2.0 markers. A governed use case without authorisation is refused under every policy, because that rule is a MUST.

One detail demonstrators usually get wrong. The Swiss Profile's SHOULD-level trust rules cannot be satisfied in the Sandbox, where actors have no identity onboarding. Rather than deleting them, the Sandbox policy records them as waived — the journal shows which rule was relaxed and why. A demo that silently drops rules teaches that the rules are optional.

And the journal itself holds claim names, never claim values. It proves an interaction stayed within the rules without quietly becoming a second copy of the patient's data. A test asserts that no AHV number and no vaccine lot number ever reaches it.

Standards

Reuse the clinical models. Decline the repository.

openEHR and HL7 FHIR are normally adopted as a package: shared information models and a shared repository someone operates, governs, and the patient does not control. This project takes the first and declines the second.

Every claim carries its FHIR element path and, where one exists, its openEHR archetype path. At the moment of presentation the receiving system rebuilds the representation it already understands — locally, from what the holder released, with no CDR and no FHIR server on either side.

Two properties are load-bearing and are stated wherever the code does it. A projection is derived, never authoritative — the signed credential stays the evidence. And it is legitimately partial: after selective disclosure a resource may have findings and no patient name, and receiving systems have to accept a missing element as a legitimate outcome. That is the real integration cost of this approach.

Honest position

What is not solved

No one can grant the roles yet

Nothing here deploys until some body can issue the trust statement that says "this DID is a practice authorised to vaccinate". The Confederation's onboarding verifies who an organisation is; it does not say what it may do in healthcare. No health-domain governance body exists to say that. The technology is ready some distance ahead of the institutional arrangements — that is the headline finding of step 1, and no amount of code closes it.

Series across issuers

Dose 2 is given by a pharmacy that cannot see dose 1. Today the administering party asserts the dose number, so a wallet holding two "dose 1 of 3" credentials is possible. Resolving it needs a presentation at administration time or a shared series identifier.

The double-dispensing window

Between a prescription being presented and being revoked there is a window in which a second pharmacy could accept it. Paper has the same window and closes it physically.

"Protected against X" is an inference

The credential says which diseases a dose targets and when. Whether that amounts to protection depends on the schedule and elapsed time. Who is accountable for that inference is a clinical-safety question for the sector to settle.

Presentations are not unlinkable

A credential presented twice is the same credential. Batch issuance mitigates this but is not used here, because with a dose series the claim values are themselves close to identifying.

Public health still needs numbers

A fully decentralised record does not produce coverage statistics as a side effect. Consent-based secondary use is sketched in F-09; it is not a substitute for surveillance, and saying otherwise would be the weakest claim in this blueprint.
